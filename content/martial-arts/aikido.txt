AIKIDO
"Win by not meeting force with force."

SNAPSHOT
  Origin:      Japan
  Category:    Grappling
  Difficulty:  Advanced
  Focus:       Joint locks, throws, redirection, weapons basics

WHAT IT IS
Aikido ("the way of harmonizing energy") is a Japanese art that deals with an
attack by blending with its direction and redirecting it, using joint locks and
throws instead of strikes. It prizes control, balance, and de-escalation, with
the aim of ending a conflict while keeping harm to everyone, including the
attacker, as low as possible. It is subtle, and it takes years to make work.

HISTORY
Morihei Ueshiba ("O-Sensei") created aikido in Japan in the early to mid 20th
century. He drew on Daito-ryu aikijujutsu, sword and spear arts, and his own
spiritual beliefs, especially those of the Omoto religion. After the Second
World War the art grew through his students, who founded the main branches
such as Aikikai, Yoshinkan, and Ki Society. Schools differ in how hard and how
realistic their training is.

WHAT TRAINING LOOKS LIKE
  - Practice is paired. One partner (tori) responds to a set attack from the
    other (uke), then they swap roles.
  - Ukemi: rolling and falling safely, which takes up a lot of class time.
  - Attacks include grabs, strikes, and holds, answered by throws or pins.
  - Weapons work with the bokken (wooden sword), jo (staff), and tanto (knife).
  - Most schools do not hold competitions or free sparring, though a few
    (such as Tomiki-style) do.

CORE SKILLS
  Joint locks   Controlling wrists, elbows, and shoulders.
  Throws        Using momentum and off-balancing, not strength.
  Redirection   Entering and turning to move out of the line of attack.
  Weapons       Sword, staff, and knife basics that inform the empty-hand work.
  Ukemi         Safe, smooth falling and rolling.

STRENGTHS
  - Low-impact, so suitable for many ages and fitness levels.
  - Emphasizes de-escalation, awareness, and calm.
  - Improves balance, posture, flexibility, and body control.
  - Develops sensitivity to timing and distance.

LIMITATIONS
  - Steep learning curve, and slow before it begins to feel natural.
  - Cooperative practice with little resisting makes it hard to know what
    works under real pressure.
  - Lacks the striking and ground work that other arts include.
  - Quality and realism differ widely between schools.

SELF-DEFENSE VALUE
Its principles of avoiding force, keeping distance, and controlling gently are
valuable, especially for de-escalation. As a standalone way to defend against
a fast, resisting attacker, it is weaker unless the school trains against
resistance. Many practitioners cross-train.

GOOD FIT FOR
Patient people who value long-term development, calm, and low-impact training.
Less suited to anyone wanting quick results or hard sparring.
