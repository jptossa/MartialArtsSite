ESKRIMA
"Learn it with a stick. It all transfers to your hands."

SNAPSHOT
  Origin:      Philippines
  Category:    Weapons
  Difficulty:  Intermediate
  Focus:       Stick and blade work, footwork, flow drills, empty-hand defense

WHAT IT IS
Eskrima is a Filipino martial art centered on weapons: sticks, blades, and
improvised objects. It is also known as kali or arnis, depending on the region
and school. Training starts with a rattan stick and teaches a set of angles,
strikes, and blocks, then applies the same movement to knives and empty hands.
Because the footwork and principles are shared, skills with one weapon carry
over to the others.

HISTORY
Eskrima developed across the Philippine islands over centuries, shaped by local
fighting traditions and by contact with Spanish colonizers, whose influence is
visible in some of the art's vocabulary. It was practiced in secret during
parts of the colonial period. Today many schools and lineages exist, and the
art is used by some military and police training programs and appears in
fighting choreography for film.

WHAT TRAINING LOOKS LIKE
  - Warm-up and footwork drills, often in triangle or box patterns.
  - Stick drills with a partner, such as sinawali, a flowing weaving pattern.
  - Learning strikes by numbered angles, then combining them in sequences.
  - Controlled sparring with padded sticks and protective gear.
  - Knife work and empty-hand techniques taught as variations on the same
    movement. Most practice uses wooden or training knives.

CORE SKILLS
  Angles       Numbered strikes and the lines of attack they follow.
  Footwork     Moving in, out, and around an opponent to manage distance.
  Flow drills  Continuous partner patterns that build rhythm and coordination.
  Blade work   Using and defending against knives with training tools.
  Empty hands  Trapping, checking, and striking, using the same movement
               patterns.

STRENGTHS
  - Builds coordination, timing, and hand speed.
  - Teaches awareness of improvised weapons and distance in armed situations.
  - One set of principles applies to sticks, knives, and empty hands.
  - Low-impact, partner-based practice, with relatively modest equipment needs.

LIMITATIONS
  - Fewer schools than mainstream arts, and quality varies widely by lineage
    and instructor.
  - Little or no focus on grappling or ground fighting.
  - Flow drills can be cooperative, so check that a school also uses
    pressure-testing and resisting partners.
  - Weapon training does not, on its own, prepare you for an unarmed fight.
    Laws about carrying weapons differ by place, so ask a qualified person
    about the rules where you live.

SELF-DEFENSE VALUE
Useful for understanding how armed attacks work and how to manage distance,
with strong hand-speed and coordination benefits. For safety, the best advice
against a weapon is still to avoid, escape, and get help when possible.

GOOD FIT FOR
People curious about weapons-based martial arts, anyone who likes flowing,
rhythmic partner training, and students who want to build coordination and
timing.
