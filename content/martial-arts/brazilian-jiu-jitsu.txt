BRAZILIAN JIU-JITSU
"Chess, but someone is sitting on you."

SNAPSHOT
  Origin:      Brazil
  Category:    Grappling
  Difficulty:  Intermediate
  Focus:       Ground control, joint locks, chokes, escapes

WHAT IT IS
Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (BJJ) is a ground-fighting art built on leverage,
positioning, and timing. The central idea is that a smaller, weaker person can
control and submit a larger, stronger opponent by using proper technique
instead of strength. Most of the work happens after the fight has gone to the
ground, and the goal is to control the opponent, not to hurt them.

HISTORY
BJJ grew out of Japanese jujutsu and judo, which Mitsuyo Maeda taught in Brazil
in the early 1900s. The Gracie family, especially Carlos and Helio Gracie,
adapted those techniques in the following decades, putting extra weight on
ground fighting and leverage for smaller practitioners. The art reached a
worldwide audience in the 1990s through early mixed martial arts events, where
it showed how effective ground control could be.

WHAT TRAINING LOOKS LIKE
  - Warm-up drills such as shrimping, bridging, and technical stand-ups.
  - A technique of the day, shown by the instructor and drilled with a partner.
  - "Rolling": live sparring from the knees or from standing, at a pace you
    control.
  - Classes are usually run in a gi (jacket and pants) or no-gi (shorts and a
    rash guard). Belts run white, blue, purple, brown, black, and promotion
    is slow, often years per belt.

CORE SKILLS
  Ground control   Guard, mount, side control, and back control, and how to
                   move between them.
  Joint locks      Armbars, kimuras, and leg locks, applied to the point of
                   submission and no further.
  Chokes           Rear-naked, triangle, and cross-collar chokes, which end a
                   fight by restricting blood flow or the airway.
  Escapes          Getting out from underneath: bridging, framing, and
                   recovering guard. A large share of early training.

STRENGTHS
  - Works regardless of size, because technique stands in for strength.
  - Sparring can be done at full intensity with low injury risk, since
    submissions are practiced with a tap-out rule.
  - A demanding full-body workout.
  - Teaches calm problem-solving while under physical pressure.

LIMITATIONS
  - Most of the art assumes one opponent and a ground fight. Staying on the
    ground in a street situation with several attackers is risky.
  - Takedowns and standing defense get less attention in some schools.
  - Skin infections and joint or finger strain are common minor issues.
    Hygiene and tapping early matter.

SELF-DEFENSE VALUE
BJJ is among the best-tested arts for dealing with a single aggressor who has
grabbed you or taken you down. It teaches you to stay composed while someone
heavy is on top of you. For real-world use, pair it with awareness, exits, and
some striking or takedown training so the plan is to get up and leave.

GOOD FIT FOR
Patient, analytical people, smaller people looking for a size equalizer, and
anyone who prefers live practice to forms. Not ideal if you dislike close
physical contact.
